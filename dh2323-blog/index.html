Phase 1

We didn't start from scratch. We followed QuantitativeBytes' qbRayTrace series: a full YouTube tutorial that builds a C++ ray tracer one feature at a time, so you can type along from an empty project. Open in another tab: tinyraytracer. It crams the same ideas into 200 lines with no abstraction layer, which often made it the fastest place to figure out what some particular trick is actually doing.

Once it was running, we played with it. Moved spheres around, changed light directions, tweaked the refraction IOR. The limitations of the tutorial-grade renderer became obvious fast: spheres only; strict pinhole, no aperture; every parameter change wants a recompile, then a re-run, then opening the PPM, so a single tweak takes seconds; and rendering is single-threaded, so anything beyond a small image makes you wait. All of those were already on the to-fix list for Phases 2 and 3.

The basic idea, in one paragraph

Now that we'd worked through the tutorial, here's how we'd put it. Every pixel on the screen is a line of sight from your eye, through that pixel, into the scene. Ray tracing reverses it: shoot a ray from the camera, see where in the scene it hits, and figure out what color that hit point should be.

A ray is just a parametrized line with origin o and direction d. Any point on it:

P(t) = o + t · d   (t > 0) ray equation

To find the hit, you analytically intersect this ray with each object in the scene. In the tutorial-grade version those are spheres, so the math is just a quadratic. Take the smallest positive t and that's the first thing the camera sees through this pixel.

Why the recursion shows up

Once you have the hit, you need its color. For a rough diffuse surface, classic shading does it: Blinn–Phong (ambient + diffuse + specular), plus an extra shadow ray toward the light to check whether something blocks it.

But if you hit a mirror or glass, one ray isn't enough. A mirror spawns a reflection ray (law of reflection); glass spawns a refraction ray (Snell's law). Then you run the whole thing recursively on that new ray (intersect, shade, possibly spawn more rays...) and fold the returned color back in. That's a Whitted-style ray tracer, basically what Whitted described in 1980, and it's what most teaching projects implement. The whole thing is roughly:

L(hit) = direct + kr · L(reflected ray) + kt · L(refracted ray) Whitted-style recursion

kr and kt are the reflection and refraction coefficients (for glass these come from Fresnel). Cap the recursion depth somewhere reasonable, say 5 bounces, and stop.

That's all of Phase 1, basically. There's no global illumination here: no indirect light, no soft shadows, no caustics. Those don't fit in Whitted's framework. For the full picture you need Kajiya's 1986 rendering equation, which says all light is an integral, plus Monte Carlo path tracing to approximate it. But Whitted-style was enough for what we wanted to do: depth of field, not full global illumination. Phase 2 swaps the camera model from pinhole to thin lens.

Phase 2

Phase 2 left the tutorial behind. We wrote our own C++ renderer, and it was the first part of the project that's actually ours. Two things drove it: swap the pinhole camera for a thin-lens model so depth of field can show up naturally, and tighten the "tweak parameter, look at result" loop as much as we could.

Thin-lens camera model

A pinhole camera is always sharp everywhere because there's no aperture. Thin lens is different: every chief ray's origin is randomly sampled on a disk (the aperture), and they all converge on the same point on the focal plane. The bigger the aperture, the more an off-focus object gets hit at different image positions by different sample paths. That's depth-of-field blur.

CoC-driven adaptive sampling

Thin-lens means each pixel needs multiple rays. But really, most pixels don't: a pixel right on the focal plane is going to be sharp anyway, and resampling it only gives you the same sharp result. So we did a simple thing. Use one chief ray to estimate how blurry each pixel should be (via the circle of confusion, CoC), then assign a sample count: 4 rays near the focal plane, 8 in the transition, 16 in the heavily defocused region.

Three camera modes

The top of the UI has Camera Mode as three big side-by-side buttons. Three different points on the quality-vs-speed curve:

· Pinhole

1 spp, no aperture, one ray per pixel. Always fully sharp, no depth of field, fastest of the three. We mostly used it for instant preview while moving the camera and framing shots.

· Fixed sample

Same number of rays per pixel (4 / 8 / 16). Bokeh is uniformly smooth, so it's a good "ceiling" reference. Downside: pixels near the focal plane that are already sharp still get resampled, so the budget gets wasted on them.

· Progressive lens sampling (CoC adaptive)

The 4 / 8 / 16 spp adaptive scheme from above. This is the default. In most scenes it's visually close to 16 spp Fixed, with about half the total rays.

The little UI for tuning parameters

The C++ renderer only runs from the command line, so for tuning we built a small Tkinter window in Python: Camera Mode buttons across the top, two side-by-side canvases for the rendered image and CoC heatmap, sliders along the bottom, Full Quality / Re-render / Start Record / Export MP4 buttons in the bottom-left corner. A slider move triggers a Python thread that runs the C++ binary, reads the output back as an image, and refreshes the canvas. C++ rendering itself is OpenMP-parallel and reasonably fast. But spawning a fresh subprocess every time you touch a slider has a fixed cost. Tens of milliseconds go into process startup and file I/O before any rendering begins. You drop frames before the first ray is even shot.

Phase 3

Phase 3 needed to do two things at once. Support arbitrary triangle meshes, not just spheres. And kill the per-frame subprocess overhead from Phase 2 by running the renderer directly inside Unity.

Triangle meshes and BVH

A spheres-only renderer can't run a real demo scene. So we added two things: ray-triangle intersection (the classic Möller–Trumbore), and a BVH so we don't test every triangle. The BVH uses the Surface Area Heuristic to pick splits, plus the slab method for AABB intersection. Scenes with tens of thousands of triangles can still hit interactive frame rates.

Wiring C++ into Unity

The Phase 2 "spawn a subprocess and read PPM" approach got cut entirely. We compiled the C++ core into a DLL and called its render function directly from a Unity C# script via P/Invoke. The trick is to "pin" the pixel buffer on the C# side (so the GC won't move it), hand the pointer to C++, and have C++ write RGBA straight into that memory. Nothing copies across the managed/native boundary. Unity then uploads that byte array as a GPU texture.

Click-to-focus interaction

The last addition is the feature we ended up using the most. DSLR-style click-to-focus. A Unity Physics.Raycast measures the distance to whatever the cursor is over, and a click syncs that distance into the ray tracer's focus distance parameter. A low-frequency probe ray runs in the background while the cursor moves: hover over an object, and a small reticle in the center of the screen turns green to say "this can be focused on".

CoC metric and lens sampling, cleaned up

We also cleaned up the CoC estimator. Out went the Phase 2 form (a direct ratio on distance), in came the inverse-depth form:

CoC(z) = a · | 1/z − 1/df | inverse-depth form

a is the aperture radius, df is the focus distance. This form returns exactly 0 on the focal plane and is just cleaner to write. Lens disk sampling also moved from the Phase 2 Halton + golden-angle hybrid to pure Vogel sampling, so bokeh stays smoother at low spp and you get fewer ring artifacts.

The final demo

The video below walks through all three camera modes inside the demo scene. The interaction is the same in every mode: a click on the image is the "shutter" (it measures focus distance), and the UI lets you tweak aperture radius and focus distance live. What differs is what those parameters do:

  • Progressive: 4/8/16 spp assigned by CoC. Toggle the heatmap and you can see exactly which tier each pixel landed in, and watch the distribution shift as you move the thresholds.
  • Fixed: same sample count for every pixel, regardless of CoC. Threshold sliders do nothing here. Bokeh is uniformly smooth and serves as a quality reference.
  • Pinhole: pan-focus, no depth of field. Always sharp, fastest. Aperture and focus distance have no visible effect in this mode.

Reflections

Looking back, the hardest stretch wasn't Phase 1, and it wasn't Phase 2: it was the jump from Phase 2 to Phase 3. Two big independent changes (arbitrary triangle meshes via BVH + Möller–Trumbore, and moving the renderer from a separate process into a Unity native plugin) landed at the same time, and a bug in either would dress itself up as a bug in the other. The first few days of Phase 3 were the most fragile period of the whole project. More generally: each module of a ray tracer is not that complicated by itself, but once coordinate frames, normal directions, ray biasing, and recursion termination are all chained together, it gets very easy to break something subtle. The image just looks "a bit off", and you can't tell where. What got us through was forcing every frame to be inspectable layer by layer (hit depth, normal, CoC, sampling tier). Once we could see each stage in isolation, the pipeline stopped being mysterious.

The engineering side was mostly cross-language details. Unity occasionally caches a stale DLL, and when it does, the symptom likes to dress up as "BVH is broken" or "the glass branch has a bug". At the P/Invoke boundary, if the pixel buffer wasn't properly pinned, you'd get sporadic visual corruption and crashes that were a pain to reproduce. One particular bug we still remember: an early Phase 2 version of the depth-of-field code biased ray origins along the camera's world-forward axis instead of the ray direction. That broke lens-sample coplanarity and showed up as faint ghosting at silhouettes. The fix was one line. Finding it took most of an evening. DLL version checking, a strict pinned-memory rule, and that one patient evening between them got the rest of these problems off our backs.

User feedback and a perceptual angle

We didn't run a formal perceptual user study. Late in development we just asked some classmates and friends to try the Unity build and tell us what they thought. Two features got the most positive reactions: click-to-focus and the split-view comparison. Most people said the same kind of thing, namely that once they could literally click on something to pull focus to it, depth of field stopped being an abstract math concept and turned into something they could see and play with. A couple of users also pointed out that under wide apertures with strong defocus, bokeh in the far background still looked a bit noisy, and asked if the system could "just sample a bit more there". That observation lines up with the CoC threshold sensitivity we hit ourselves, and it suggests an obvious next step: replace the fixed thresholds with a perceptual-metric-based online estimator. Something like FLIP for the objective reference, plus a 2AFC paradigm to compare different sampling strategies against just-noticeable-difference thresholds. That would put the "compute by perception" idea on a quantitative footing. Beyond what we could do this semester, but it's the natural follow-up.

Use of AI tools

We used AI mainly for two things on this project. First, cross-checking the report against the code, i.e. making sure the writeup actually matches what the implementation does, including the formulas. That caught a few errors in our descriptions: the BVH partitioning method, the ray branching in the glass material, and the form of the CoC formula. Second, for language polishing on the report and the code comments.
